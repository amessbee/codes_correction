Mission briefing

Meet NILA

A small robot, a frozen ocean, and one very expensive flashlight.

What can go wrong 628 million km from home?AURORA / NILA-1

Explain the concrete fiction: NILA landed near Europa’s Conamara Chaos region, drills shallow ice cores, warms samples, and searches for chemical or microscopic patterns. Avoid claiming that it has found life.

NASA leads the fictional AURORA mission with international partners. The story is invented; the communication constraints and coding ideas are realistic.

Invite three quick hazards: low battery, radiation, blocked line of sight, broken equipment, noisy signals.

NILA → LUMEN → Earth
One-way journey: tens of minutes
Why not simply ask NILA to resend?Line of sight matters

NILA cannot aim a powerful radio dish at Earth. It flashes a visible-light laser to LUMEN, which orbits Europa. LUMEN forwards the message by radio to Earth.

NILA wakes twice per Earth day. LUMEN is usefully positioned for only about 90 seconds. If a report is unclear, waiting for another window costs half a day and valuable energy.

Ask why low latency is not the only issue: probe lifetime, battery, changing conditions, and scientific priority all matter.

Why is two copies not enough?Works, works, works… but costs

Two copies can reveal disagreement but cannot tell which copy is correct. Three copies allow a majority vote if at most one transmitted light among the three copies of each position is wrong.

Repeating the five-bit report three times costs fifteen flashes, beyond the mission budget of twelve.

Introduce redundancy: deliberate extra information used for reliability. The research question is not whether to use redundancy, but how efficiently.

Start from a five-bit message. Add one parity light so the total number of greens is even. Let students toggle the lights and keep the watchdog satisfied.

Flip any single light. The total parity changes from even to odd, so Earth detects an error.

Then ask which light was wrong. One parity check cannot identify the location. Suggested joke: “The watchdog knows somebody entered the house, but cannot tell which window.”

Vocabulary: error detection is not the same as error correction.

How far apart must valid messages be?Count differing positions

Define distance only after students count the different positions. Hamming distance is the number of coordinates where two strings differ.

If valid messages have distance one, a single flip can turn one valid message into another. At distance two, a corrupted word can sit equally close to both. At distance three, every one-error version remains uniquely closest to its original.

Use the islands metaphor: a one-bit error moves one step. Error “bubbles” around valid messages must not overlap.

Research door 1 · Pack the message bubbles
A counting lower bound

Each protected message occupies:

1 original + n one-flip neighbours
20(n + 1) ≤ 2n
Try n = 7, 8, 9. What does counting prove—and what does it not prove?
Could eight flashes be enough?Hamming / sphere-packing bound

Group exercise

For length n, a word plus all words one flip away has size n+1. Twenty correctable messages need twenty disjoint balls, inside 2ⁿ total strings.

  • n = 7: 20×8 = 160 > 128, so impossible.
  • n = 8: 20×9 = 180 ≤ 256, so counting does not rule it out.
  • n = 9: 20×10 = 200 ≤ 512; our forthcoming construction uses nine.

Emphasize the research lesson: a lower bound says what cannot work; it may not provide a construction. Determining the largest code with given length and distance is written A₂(n,d).

Extension: model all n-bit words as graph vertices, join pairs at distance 1 or 2, and seek a largest independent set.

A nine-flash repair code
4 inspectors · 5 data lights
Why powers of two: 1, 2, 4, 8?Every position gets a unique inspector signature

Place check lights in positions 1, 2, 4, and 8. Data occupies 3, 5, 6, 7, 9. Each position number has a unique binary description, which tells which inspectors watch it.

  • Inspector 1 watches odd-numbered positions: 1,3,5,7,9.
  • Inspector 2 watches 2,3,6,7.
  • Inspector 4 watches 4,5,6,7.
  • Inspector 8 watches 8,9.

Each inspector enforces even parity on its group. A damaged position triggers exactly the inspectors whose labels add to that position number.

For younger groups, treat this as a detective game. For older groups, connect the pattern to binary place values.

Assign one inspector to each group. Put data 1,0,0,1,0 in positions 3,5,6,7,9. Each group calculates its check light.

Results for even parity: p1=0, p2=0, p4=1, p8=0. The nine-bit codeword is 001100100.

Use “Next step” to reveal data placement, then check values. Do not rush the arithmetic; let students physically count green lights in each inspector’s set.

Detection and correction are different promisesSECDED

A distance-3 Hamming code can miscorrect two errors because the syndrome may point to an innocent position. Add a tenth overall parity bit to obtain single-error correction, double-error detection (SECDED).

Challenge table

  • Syndrome 0, overall parity OK: no error.
  • Syndrome nonzero, overall parity wrong: one error in indicated position.
  • Syndrome 0, overall parity wrong: error in the overall parity bit.
  • Syndrome nonzero, overall parity OK: two errors detected; do not “repair.”

Ask what the probe should do after detecting but not correcting two errors: request retransmission, mark data uncertain, or use a stronger code.

Research door 3 · Is nine optimal?
20 messages · correct 1 flip
n = 7 → impossible
n = 8 → not ruled out
n = 9 → construction found
Construct 20 eight-bit words, each pair at distance ≥ 3—or prove it cannot be done.
This is a genuine finite research problemA₂(8,3)

We have a nine-bit construction, and counting proves seven bits cannot work. Eight remains the interesting gap after the elementary argument.

Translate it into several languages:

  • Coding: choose twenty length-8 binary words with minimum distance 3.
  • Graph theory: from the graph on 256 words, choose twenty vertices with no pair at distance 1 or 2.
  • Optimization: maximum independent set / integer programming.
  • Computation: greedy search, backtracking, SAT, or symmetry breaking.

Do not require a full solution in class. A good mini-project is to find the largest code students can construct and provide a verifiable distance table.

Suggested deliverables

  • A one-page problem statement defining message length, alphabet, and allowed errors.
  • A construction or algorithm.
  • A verifier that checks every pair or every error pattern.
  • A comparison with a simple bound or baseline.
  • One unanswered question discovered during the work.

Age differentiation

Grades 6–7 can build physical codebooks and test them. Grades 8–9 can write a small brute-force checker. Grade 10 can formulate graphs, matrices, or optimization models.

Important research habit: distinguish random noise, known erasures, bursts, insertions/deletions, and adversarial errors. Different channels demand different codes.
