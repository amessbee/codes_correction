Mission briefing
Year 2090
Near-Ice Life Analyzer

Meet Near-Ice Life Analyzer (NILA)

A small robot, a frozen ocean, and one very expensive flashlight.

What can go wrong 628 million km from home?AURORA / NILA-1

Explain the concrete fiction: NILA landed near Europa’s Conamara Chaos region, drills shallow ice cores, warms samples, and searches for chemical or microscopic patterns. Avoid claiming that it has found life.

NASA leads the fictional AURORA mission with international partners. The story is invented; the communication constraints and coding ideas are realistic.

Invite three quick hazards: low battery, radiation, blocked line of sight, broken equipment, noisy signals.

NILA → LUMEN → Earth
One-way journey: tens of minutes
Why not simply ask NILA to resend?Line of sight matters

NILA cannot aim a powerful radio dish at Earth. It flashes a visible-light laser to LUMEN, which orbits Europa. LUMEN forwards the message by radio to Earth.

NILA wakes twice per Earth day. LUMEN is usefully positioned for only about 90 seconds. If a report is unclear, waiting for another window costs half a day and valuable energy.

Ask why low latency is not the only issue: probe lifetime, battery, changing conditions, and scientific priority all matter.

Why is two copies not enough?Works, works, works… but costs

Two copies can reveal disagreement but cannot tell which copy is correct. Three copies allow a majority vote if at most one transmitted light among the three copies of each position is wrong.

Repeating the four-bit report three times costs twelve flashes, beyond the mission budget of eight.

Introduce redundancy: deliberate extra information used for reliability. The research question is not whether to use redundancy, but how efficiently.

How far apart must valid messages be?Open closest-neighbor graph

Define distance only after students count the different positions. Hamming distance is the number of coordinates where two strings differ.

If valid messages have distance one, a single flip can turn one valid message into another. At distance two, a corrupted word can sit equally close to both. At distance three, every one-error version remains uniquely closest to its original.

Use the islands metaphor: a one-bit error moves one step. Error “bubbles” around valid messages must not overlap.

Treat this as a genuine attempt, but grant it every advantage: assume the first neighborhoods never overlap accidentally.

Each protected five-bit message needs six received strings: the original and the five strings reached by one flip. Five such groups use 30 of the 32 strings. Only two remain, but message six needs six.

Therefore five flashes cannot protect sixteen reports, regardless of how cleverly the legal messages are chosen.

Could seven flashes be enough?Hamming / sphere-packing bound

Group exercise

For length n, a word plus all words one flip away has size n+1. Sixteen correctable messages need sixteen disjoint balls, inside 2ⁿ total strings.

  • n = 6: 16×7 = 112 > 64, so impossible.
  • n = 7: 16×8 = 128 = 128 — an exact fit, nothing wasted.

The Hamming(7,4) code built next meets this bound with equality: a perfect code. Emphasize the research lesson: a lower bound says what cannot work; here it is tight. Determining the largest code with given length and distance is written A₂(n,d).

Extension: model all n-bit words as graph vertices, join pairs at distance 1 or 2, and seek a largest independent set.

Detection and correction are different promisesSECDED

A distance-3 Hamming code can miscorrect two errors because the syndrome may point to an innocent position. Add an eighth overall parity bit to obtain single-error correction, double-error detection (SECDED).

Challenge table

  • Syndrome 0, overall parity OK: no error.
  • Syndrome nonzero, overall parity wrong: one error in indicated position.
  • Syndrome 0, overall parity wrong: error in the overall parity bit.
  • Syndrome nonzero, overall parity OK: two errors detected; do not “repair.”

Ask what the probe should do after detecting but not correcting two errors: request retransmission, mark data uncertain, or use a stronger code.

Research door 3 · Is seven optimal?
16 messages · correct 1 flip
n = 6 → impossible
n = 7 → perfect fit
16 · 8 = 128 = 27
Add a 17th report, or demand two-error correction. When does counting stop deciding it?
This is a genuine finite research problemA₂(7,3) = 16

For sixteen messages the counting bound is tight: n = 6 is impossible, and n = 7 fits with equality. Hamming(7,4) is a perfect code, so seven flashes is provably optimal.

Push the numbers and the problem reopens. Translate it into several languages:

  • Coding: choose seventeen length-7 binary words with minimum distance 3 — or prove it is impossible.
  • Graph theory: from the graph on 128 words, choose seventeen vertices with no pair at distance 1 or 2.
  • Optimization: maximum independent set / integer programming.
  • Computation: greedy search, backtracking, SAT, or symmetry breaking.

Do not require a full solution in class. A good mini-project is to find the largest code students can construct and provide a verifiable distance table.

Age differentiation
